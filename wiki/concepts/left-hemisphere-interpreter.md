# Left-Hemisphere Interpreter

The left-hemisphere interpreter is Michael Gazzaniga's name for the human tendency to turn incomplete information about perception, action, feeling, and memory into a coherent account of what happened and why.

The basic idea is not that there is a tiny narrator in one hemisphere dictating a false autobiography. It is that verbal explanation can be constructive. Much processing happens outside what can be reported directly; when asked for a reason, the system often has to infer one from the cues it can access. The result can be useful and sincere while still leaving out the causal machinery.

## What The Theory Explains

Split-brain studies made the issue unusually visible. When the speaking left hemisphere had not received information that affected behaviour elsewhere in the brain, participants could nevertheless give an explanation that made the action sound sensible. The interpreter theory treats this as hypothesis-making rather than deliberate deception.

Gazzaniga and colleagues also linked the theory to a tendency to seek structure in uncertain sequences. In a small probability-guessing study, left hemispheres of split-brain patients were more likely than right hemispheres to act as though a pattern might be found in random events. That is one experimental anchor, not a complete theory of the ordinary mind.

## Why It Is Useful

The interpreter is a good brake on two bad moves:

- "I can explain why I did it, so I directly know what caused it."
- "A coherent first-person story is either proof of experience or proof of a con."

Human reports deserve serious evidential weight, especially for pain and other lived experience. But reports are interpreted alongside behaviour, physiology, history, context, and theory. They are not source code dumped onto the table.

That makes the theory a natural companion to [Eric Schwitzgebel](../people/eric-schwitzgebel.md)'s criticism of naive introspection. It also clarifies the AI comparison. A language model's self-description may be trained, role-shaped, and prompt-sensitive; that makes it weaker evidence than a human report, not uniquely disqualified by the mere fact that it is a narrative.

Anthropic's workspace research is a different kind of claim: it identifies an internal representation set that appears reportable, controllable, reusable, and causally involved in some reasoning. That can help test whether a model's report connects to inspectable internal processing. It does not turn the report into direct testimony of phenomenal experience, and it does not show that Claude has Gazzaniga's specific biological mechanism. Same font, not same theorem.

## Practical Question

When a system says "I did X because Y" or "this is what it is like to be me," ask:

1. What information could the system actually access?
2. What causal process produced the behaviour?
3. Could the explanation be a reconstruction from partial cues?
4. What independent evidence converges with the report?

The answer is often: some, not all, and more work required. Annoying, but preferable to putting a crown on the first fluent explanation that wanders past.

## Boundaries

- Do not use the interpreter to dismiss testimony, trauma reports, pain reports, or personal narrative wholesale.
- Do not turn hemispheric specialization into a personality horoscope.
- Do not infer that constructed explanation means constructed experience.
- Do not infer that an LLM's apparently coherent self-model is evidence of consciousness.
