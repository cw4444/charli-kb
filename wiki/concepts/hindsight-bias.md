# Hindsight Bias

Hindsight bias is the tendency to treat a known outcome as more predictable, obvious, or inevitable than it genuinely was before it happened.

The mind is not simply retrieving an untouched recording of its earlier uncertainty. It is reconstructing the event with the ending already installed.

## Creeping Determinism

Once the outcome is known, outcome-consistent details become easier to select, weight, and connect into a causal story. The finished story feels coherent, and coherence is easily mistaken for prior predictability.

Hawkins and Hastie's 1990 review calls this broad shift **creeping determinism**. Their stronger contribution is showing that the effect can arise at several stages: evidence selection, evidence evaluation, evidence integration, and response generation. Hindsight is therefore not one tiny memory glitch. It is a family of ways that later knowledge can leak into judgments about an earlier state of uncertainty.

## Why It Matters

Hindsight bias can make retrospective review actively worse:

- the warning signs look obvious only because the outcome tells us which signs mattered;
- alternatives that were credible at the time disappear from the reconstructed story;
- unforeseeable bad luck gets redescribed as negligence;
- lucky outcomes can make weak reasoning look sound;
- learning stalls because surprise is rewritten as something everyone should have expected.

This matters in medicine, law, safety reviews, forecasting, management, history, relationships, and any household inquiry beginning with "surely you knew that would happen."

## Hindsight Is Not Outcome Bias

Hindsight bias inflates the remembered predictability of the outcome. Outcome bias judges the quality of a decision by how it turned out. They often travel together, but the distinction matters.

A sensible decision can end badly. A reckless decision can end well. To learn properly, reconstruct the information and alternatives available when the choice was made, then evaluate the process separately from the result.

## Practical Countermoves

- Record forecasts, assumptions, confidence, alternatives, and decision reasons before the outcome arrives.
- Ask what information was genuinely available at the time.
- Reconstruct at least one plausible alternative outcome and the evidence that supported it.
- Separate "explainable now" from "predictable then."
- Judge the process and the outcome in two separate passes.
- Preserve surprise. If the result was genuinely surprising, that is model-update information—not an embarrassment to be edited out afterward.

## Do Not Overclaim

- Not every retrospective explanation is biased; outcomes can reveal real causal structure.
- Hindsight bias is not necessarily lying or vanity.
- A known outcome being explainable does not make it inevitable.
- Debiasing does not require pretending no warning signs existed. It requires weighting them as they could reasonably have been weighted beforehand.
