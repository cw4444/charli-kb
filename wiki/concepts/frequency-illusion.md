# Frequency Illusion

Frequency illusion is the feeling that something has suddenly started appearing everywhere after it has recently become salient.

The familiar name is the Baader-Meinhof phenomenon. Arnold Zwicky's useful research-adjacent framing is cleaner: once a phenomenon is noticed, people may believe it happens a lot. In his 2006 abstract, Zwicky treats frequency illusion as the result of selective attention and confirmation bias working together: the noticed thing becomes salient, and then supporting appearances are easier to notice than non-appearances.

## Mechanism

The practical loop is:

**new salience -> selective attention -> repeated noticing -> confirmation feeling -> stronger salience**

That is why it can feel like the outside world changed. Sometimes it did. Often the internal filter changed.

This is adjacent to [Salience Weighted Judgment](salience-weighted-judgment.md), but narrower. Salience-weighted judgment is the broad family: vivid, recent, repeated, emotionally loaded, or currently focused material gets extra weight. Frequency illusion is the specific "I keep seeing this everywhere now" version.

It also sits near [Repetition As Cognitive Method](repetition-as-cognitive-method.md). Repetition can build real learning, but it can also build familiarity, availability, and false importance. The brain is not a neutral camera. It is closer to a recommendation system that treats repeated attention as training data. Humans: magnificently stable, provided one does not inspect them too closely.

## Evidence Anchors

The strongest research anchor is still Tversky and Kahneman's availability heuristic: frequency and probability judgments are influenced by how easily examples come to mind. Frequency illusion is not identical with availability, but it is the household cousin.

Zwicky's linguistic examples make the illusion easy to see: once a person notices a usage pattern, they can start overestimating how common or recent it is, especially when relying on personal experience instead of sampled evidence.

The UC Denver explainer is useful as public-facing psychology context, not as a primary research paper. Carly Leonard's explanation puts the emphasis on attention and memory: recently important things receive more attentional processing and are therefore more likely to become consciously noticed.

Talluri, Urai, Tsetsos, Usher, and Donner's 2018 *Current Biology* paper adds a nearby evidence-weighting mechanism. In a perceptual decision task, the authors report confirmation bias through selective overweighting of choice-consistent evidence. That is not a paper about crystals or TikTok feeds. Its useful bridge is narrower: once a system has committed or leaned one way, later evidence can be weighted in a self-consistent direction.

Purohit's 2019 *Academic Radiology* piece shows the medical version as a practical anecdote: after learning about bovine aortic arch, a trainee reportedly noticed several cases in quick succession. That can be useful for learning rare patterns, but it can also bias attention toward the newly salient diagnosis and away from alternatives.

## Why It Matters Here

Frequency illusion is the mundane engine beneath many almost-mystical experiences:

- a word, paper, person, disease, crystal, model, or idea starts appearing everywhere;
- search and social feeds reinforce the category;
- every new sighting feels like evidence;
- the mind starts treating recognition as significance.

For Charli's Moldavite and Libyan desert glass incident, the useful interpretation is not "crystals are fake" or "the universe placed a purchase order." It is that aesthetic salience, repeated exposure, intention-language, celebrity-adjacent images, object beauty, and existing personal meaning can combine into a very persuasive noticing loop. A purchase can be meaningful and still be algorithm-brain assisted. Annoying, but financially relevant.

## Practical Countermoves

- Ask whether the base rate changed, or whether attention changed.
- Count boring non-events: how many times did the thing not appear?
- Check whether the feed, search terms, friends, or shopping behavior started supplying more of the thing.
- Separate "I keep noticing it" from "it is objectively common."
- Treat sudden recognition as a prompt for checking, not as proof of destiny.
- In diagnosis, research, and source triage, ask what alternatives the new salient category might be hiding.

## Do Not Overclaim

- Frequency illusion does not mean every noticed pattern is false.
- Newly noticing a real trend can be useful; the mistake is treating noticing as measurement.
- Confirmation bias and selective attention are not moral failures. They are ordinary attention machinery.
- A university explainer, medical anecdote, or shopping experience is not a controlled test of cosmic significance. Tragic, but probably for the best.
