# Question-Led Inquiry

Question-led inquiry is a practical research loop adapted from the productive pressure of the Oxford tutorial: begin with a question worth answering, unpack what it actually asks, gather evidence for competing answers, make an argument, and expose that argument to serious criticism.

It is not a syllabus-completion game. The question makes the brain a bloodhound: it gives reading a job, makes irrelevant material easier to discard, and reveals exactly what still needs explaining.

## The Loop

1. **Set a question with stakes.** Prefer a question that requires judgment, explanation, or comparison—not a fact lookup disguised as a quest. “Was Napoleon a good leader?” is workable; “When was Napoleon born?” is a date.
2. **Interrogate the wording.** What would count as *good*? Military success, institutional durability, human cost, legitimacy, or all of them? What is a *leader* responsible for? State a provisional standard before accumulating flattering anecdotes.
3. **Draft competing answers.** Write a tentative answer and the strongest rival answer. Name what evidence would change your mind.
4. **Research by discrimination.** Start broad enough to map the territory, then seek sources that distinguish the rival answers. Look for primary material and serious scholarship before commentary. Do not merely collect evidence that agrees with the first answer that made you feel clever.
5. **Make the case.** Produce a short, structured argument: claim, definitions, evidence, objections, reply, and remaining uncertainty.
6. **Put it under pressure.** Defend it to someone who knows the field, or deliberately use strong contrary scholarship to attack it. Revise the claim, not just the wording, when the objection lands.
7. **Record the next question.** A good inquiry ends with a better-bounded uncertainty, not with the ceremonial declaration that one has now Finished History.

## Why The Contrary Case Matters

The rival case is not a little ritual of fairness. It prevents a question from quietly becoming a search engine for one preferred conclusion. If the opposing view has no serious version, say why; if it does, make it strong enough that defeating it would matter.

This overlaps with [Friston et al.'s narrow rule-discovery example](../sources/friston-et-al-active-inference-artificial-reasoning.md): useful evidence is evidence that helps separate plausible models, rather than merely adding more compatible facts. The connection is methodological, not a claim that a history essay is an active-inference simulation in a waistcoat.

## What Makes It Different From Random Reading

Random reading can accumulate familiarity without forcing an explanation. A live question creates a selection rule: keep material that defines a key term, supplies evidence, challenges an assumption, or changes the answer; park the rest.

The question must still be allowed to change. If its terms collapse under inspection, rewrite it. If the available evidence cannot support the original scale of judgment, narrow the claim. A research goal is a compass, not handcuffs.

## A Small Working Template

```md
Question:
Why does this matter?
Key terms and standards:
Provisional answer:
Strongest contrary answer:
Evidence that would discriminate between them:
Best sources found:
Current argument:
Best objection:
What changed my mind / remains uncertain:
Next question:
```

## Boundaries

- The Oxford tutorial is not just independent reading; it includes real, repeated feedback from an expert. Self-study should not pretend a chatbot's agreeable summary is the same pressure test.
- A question can focus attention too aggressively. Schedule a small amount of exploratory reading and revise the question when the field shows it was malformed.
- "Research both sides" does not mean every claim has two equally credible sides. Give evidence and expertise their proper weight; do not manufacture balance for flat-earth nonsense in a tie.
- The method is a research-and-learning practice, not a substitute for formal training, clinical advice, or subject-specific methods.
