# Morewedge et al. - The Least Likely Of Times

## Why This Matters

Morewedge, Gilbert, and Wilson's 2005 paper identifies a specific way memory can distort emotional forecasts: when people call up one past example, the example that arrives first may be unusually good or bad. Unless the retrieval prompt makes that extremity obvious, they can mistake a memorable outlier for a representative case and forecast the next experience accordingly.

The useful sequence is:

**extreme memory retrieval -> unnoticed unrepresentativeness -> extreme future forecast**

This extends [Availability](kahneman-tversky-judgment-under-uncertainty.md) beyond judging how frequent or probable an event is. A readily retrieved episode can also become the emotional template for what the next event will feel like.

## The Three Studies

### Missing A Train

At Harvard Square station, 62 passengers described either one occasion when they missed a train, the worst occasion, or three occasions. People asked for any single occasion recalled an experience just as negative as those explicitly asked for the worst one. But the free-recall group predicted feeling worse if they missed their train that day than either the worst-instance group or the three-instance group.

The striking part is not merely that an awful memory was available. Both single-memory groups retrieved similarly awful examples. The group told to retrieve the worst occasion knew it was an outlier and adjusted its forecast; the free-recall group had no such warning label.

### Watching Harvard Football

Before a Harvard football game, 54 fans were asked to remember one Harvard win, the best Harvard win, or three wins; 46 usable responses remained after exclusions. Again, the ordinary single-memory prompt produced memories as positive as the explicit best-game prompt. Free recallers then predicted a better upcoming game than either people who knowingly recalled their best game or people who recalled three games.

This supplied the positive-valence version of the train result. Unnoticed selection of an unusually good memory can inflate expectation just as unnoticed selection of an unusually bad one can darken it.

### Watching The Red Sox

Before a Boston Red Sox game at Fenway Park, 117 fans were asked to recall one win, recall their best win, or make a forecast without recalling a game; 107 usable responses remained. Free recallers and best-game recallers again produced equally positive memories, but the free-recall and no-recall groups forecast greater enjoyment than the group explicitly asked for its best game.

The no-recall result is consistent with people retrieving a biased example spontaneously while forecasting. It is not direct proof of that hidden retrieval, because the researchers did not observe or measure what the no-recall participants were thinking about.

Across the three studies, the reported relationship between remembered and forecast affect was moderate for both free recallers and explicitly biased recallers. The authors interpret this as both groups using the recalled episode, with the explicitly biased group making a larger correction for its unrepresentativeness.

## What It Adds

The paper helps explain why experience does not automatically cure bad forecasting. Having a relevant past does not guarantee that the mind samples it fairly. Memory is not an actuarial table; it is a retrieval system that privileges distinctive, intense, recent, coherent, and emotionally loaded episodes.

It also separates two debiasing routes:

- **sample more than one episode:** several memories make it harder for one spectacular success or disaster to stand in for the class;
- **label the selection process:** explicitly recognizing that the retrieved episode is the best, worst, or otherwise unusual case can trigger correction.

For practical forecasting, the better question is not only "what happened last time?" It is "what happened across the last several comparable times, and which one did my memory volunteer first?"

## Later Context

Levine and Lench's 2018 review places the paper inside a broader account of predicted and remembered emotion. Prospection and retrospection share constructive episodic-memory processes, and errors arise when currently salient information is unrepresentative. The review also supplies an important correction to the older cartoon: people can predict some emotional intensities quite accurately, while duration, overall mood, changing attention, and changing appraisals create more room for error.

Morewedge and Todorov's 2012 experiments extended the atypical-example mechanism from emotional experience to predicting behaviour. Atypical past actions were more cognitively accessible and were overweighted when people predicted what an individual would do next. That is related evidence, not a direct replication of the 2005 affective-forecasting studies.

## Limits

- The three studies had 215 usable participants altogether and relied on immediate self-report ratings in narrow field settings.
- Participants forecast their feelings, but the researchers did not measure their later experienced feelings. The experiments therefore demonstrate forecast extremity, not the full size or direction of forecast error.
- The studies predate preregistration and modern open-data norms; no direct preregistered replication was identified in this research pass.
- Recalling three examples moderated forecasts in the tested tasks, but that does not prove that three memories reliably recover the true base rate in every domain.
- The journal version is closed access. Author-hosted Harvard PDFs are publicly readable, but no open reuse licence was identified; this page therefore summarizes the paper in original words.
