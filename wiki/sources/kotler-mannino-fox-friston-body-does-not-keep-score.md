# Kotler et al. - The Body Does Not Keep The Score

## Source Metadata

- Source: Steven Kotler, Michael Mannino, Glenn Fox, and Karl Friston, [*The body does not keep the score: trauma, predictive coding, and the restoration of metastability*](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnsys.2026.1812957), *Frontiers in Systems Neuroscience*, 2026-04-30.
- Access note: open-access **Opinion** article, not an original clinical study or systematic review.
- Status: a provocative predictive-processing reframing of trauma, with some cited empirical background and several explicit theoretical leaps.

## Summary

Kotler, Mannino, Fox, and Friston challenge a literal reading of the popular phrase "the body keeps the score." Their proposed alternative is that traumatic experience is not stored in non-neural bodily tissue; rather, brain-body loops can repeatedly recreate threat-related states when danger predictions are given excessive precision and incoming interoceptive or external signals are interpreted through them.

They frame chronic post-traumatic symptoms as reduced **metastability**: less flexible switching among semi-stable network configurations, more rigid threat-sensitive inference, and impaired recalibration. On this account, bodily sensations remain real and consequential, but they are part of an active feedback loop rather than a separate archive holding trauma outside neural processes.

The article then proposes that interventions which restore flexible network dynamics could help. It discusses exposure, EMDR, mindfulness, exercise, psychedelics, and flow-inducing activity as possible routes to recalibration. Its flow/metastability connection is explicitly a hypothesis: the authors state that direct PTSD evidence using established metastability measures is still missing.

## What The Paper Actually Adds

- A useful correction to crude storage metaphors: mind, brain, body, memory, action, and environment interact; bodily symptoms are not thereby evidence of a literal trauma cache in muscle or fascia.
- A predictive-coding language for a familiar clinical pattern: persistent threat expectations, arousal, attention to threat, and interpretation of arousal can form a self-reinforcing loop.
- A clear research question: can metastability, network switching, variability, or related measures become useful PTSD biomarkers and change with treatment?

## Where The Floor Gives Way

- This is a position piece. It does not establish that PTSD is principally or specifically a metastability disorder.
- The article itself notes that its key metastability citation concerns traumatic brain injury, not PTSD. Moving from one to the other is a hypothesis, not a completed bridge.
- It acknowledges that direct measurement of metastability in PTSD populations remains a critical gap. The claims about flow restoring it are correspondingly speculative.
- "The body does not keep the score" is a rhetorical correction to an overly literal folk reading of Bessel van der Kolk's title, not proof that trauma is merely cognitive, imagined, or disconnected from physiology, pain, hormones, immune function, motor habits, relationships, and material conditions.
- Do not use this article to dismiss trauma testimony, somatic symptoms, body-based treatment, or another person's recovery path. A nervous system is embodied; the disagreement is about mechanism and metaphor, not whether bodies matter.
