# If-Then Planning And Thought Suppression

Two complementary self-regulation findings answer a practical question: what should replace the instruction to simply not think, feel, or do something?

The better answer is usually a specific, observable cue paired with a next action: **if situation X happens, then I will do Y**. That is a plan, not a ban. It gives attention and behaviour somewhere to go when the unwanted thought or old response appears.

## If-Then Planning

Bieleke, Keller, and Gollwitzer's 2021 review calls these **implementation intentions**: plans that link an anticipated situation to a goal-directed response. A goal intention names a desired outcome ("I intend to write"); an implementation intention specifies a cue and response ("If it is 9 a.m. and I have made tea, then I open the document and draft for ten minutes").

The review synthesises nearly three decades of work on how those cue-response links can make action initiation more automatic, including work on flexibility, unplanned situations, endurance, and strategic information processing. The useful claim is modest: when a relevant cue appears, a well-specified plan can reduce the need to renegotiate the first step from scratch. It does not make a poorly chosen goal wise, remove practical constraints, or excuse failure to revise a plan when the world changes.

## Ironic Thought Suppression

Wang, Hagger, and Chatzisarantis's 2020 meta-analysis tested the familiar "white bear" problem: trying not to think a target thought can make it more accessible. Across 31 studies using Wegner-style suppression paradigms, it found a small overall post-suppression rebound effect (d+ = 0.19). During suppression itself, the ironic effect appeared when participants were under cognitive load; without load, suppression generally reduced the target thought relative to a deliberate-thought control.

That makes the popular version—"trying not to think about it always backfires"—too neat to survive contact with the evidence. The sturdier version is: suppression is effortful and becomes especially unreliable when attention is already busy, stressed, hurried, or otherwise loaded. The authors also found small-study-bias concerns for the rebound estimate, the cognitive-load rebound subgroup had only three studies, and most experiments were brief, externally prompted laboratory tasks. This is not a diagnosis or a treatment rule for intrusive thoughts, trauma, OCD, or anxiety.

## The Useful Combined Move

The connection is an inference across the two literatures, not a direct test of a single intervention: when an unwanted cue arrives, replace an unhelpful negative command with a prepared positive response.

- Less useful: "Do not spiral about the email."
- More usable: "If I notice myself rereading the email, then I write the one factual next action and return to the draft."
- Less useful: "Stop doom-scrolling."
- More usable: "If I open a news app after 10 p.m., then I close it and put the thought in tomorrow's capture note."

The point is not to force every feeling into a compliance protocol. It is to make one small action available at the moment a cue would otherwise recruit an old loop. If the cue-response plan keeps failing, alter the environment, reduce the demand, or change the goal; do not conclude that the person lacks moral fibre. That Victorian little goblin has caused enough paperwork.
