# Özbek, Bohn, And Berntsen - Imagining The Personal Past

## Why This Matters

An episodic counterfactual is a specific imagined personal event that could have happened in the past but did not. It is neither a memory of an experienced event nor simply a future fantasy pointed backward. That makes it the missing cognitive-science anchor for the wiki's [Psychological Time Travel And Reconstruction](../concepts/psychological-time-travel-and-reconstruction.md) bridge.

The paper gives the useful three-way distinction:

- **episodic memories:** what one takes to have happened;
- **episodic counterfactuals:** what could have happened, but did not;
- **episodic future projections:** what might happen.

The categories draw on related constructive capacities, but they are not phenomenologically interchangeable. That is precisely why “revision” needs a grown-up translation rather than an invoice to the universe.

## What The Study Did

Sixty-nine Turkish undergraduates generated nine personally important, specific events: a remembered event, an imagined past alternative, and an imagined future event at each of three temporal distances (about one month, one year, and five or more years). They wrote brief descriptions and rated characteristics including sensory imagery, emotional valence and intensity, perspective, rehearsal, specificity, importance, and centrality to life story and identity.

This was a within-participant phenomenology study of self-generated reports, not an experiment that implanted memories, measured memory accuracy, or tested a revision intervention.

## Findings

- Memories were easier to bring to mind, richer in sensory detail, and more often experienced from a field perspective than either counterfactuals or future projections.
- Future projections were more positive, more voluntarily rehearsed, and more central to life story and identity than either memories or counterfactuals.
- Counterfactuals occupied their own awkward middle territory: they lacked both the future events’ positivity bias and the actual memories’ enhanced sensory detail.
- With greater temporal distance, sensory detail fell while importance, cultural life-script reference, and identity centrality rose across event types.

The authors interpret the differences as consistent with reality-monitoring accounts: imagined events and remembered experiences can feel different, even when both are constructed mental scenes. They also argue that future projections’ distinctive positivity and identity centrality fit motivational accounts of future thinking.

## Why It Fits Charli's Bridge

The paper supports a narrow but useful claim: a person can mentally revisit the personal past in three different modes--remembering, imagining an alternative, and projecting forward--and those modes have different felt signatures and identity roles.

That makes Eric Cavalcanti’s line a good commentary companion: some lived experiences leave no accessible remembered record, while some remembered scenes concern events that did not occur. But the line is a philosophical/memory observation, not a result of this study. Özbek et al. does **not** show that people cannot distinguish memory from imagination, or that every vivid counterfactual becomes a false memory.

It also makes [Van Dijk et al.'s Emotional Time Travel](van-dijk-emotional-time-travel.md) more legible. Van Dijk studies a future-forecasting error caused partly by under-modelling later regulation; Özbek et al. maps the distinct phenomenology of past memory, imagined past alternatives, and imagined futures. One is about the content and feel of mental time-travel modes; the other is about a particular affective forecasting error. Neighbours, not duplicates.

## Limits

- One small, young, Turkish undergraduate sample (N = 69), using self-generated events and self-report ratings.
- The study describes phenomenology; it does not establish neural mechanisms, clinical effects, causal functions, or accuracy of autobiographical memory.
- “More sensory detail” is a reported characteristic, not a guarantee that a memory is true.
- The 2017 article is publicly readable as an author-accepted manuscript, but no open reuse licence was identified; this note is original summary, not a replacement copy.
