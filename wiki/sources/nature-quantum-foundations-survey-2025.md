# Nature Quantum Foundations Survey 2025

On 30 July 2025, *Nature* published Elizabeth Gibney's news feature, ["Physicists disagree wildly on what quantum mechanics says about reality, Nature survey shows"](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-025-02342-y). The feature is paywalled, but its questionnaire, methodology, anonymized response data, and a related open preprint are public. That is enough to preserve the useful result without reproducing the article.

## What Was Surveyed

*Nature* e-mailed 15,582 lead authors of recent, relevant research papers and invited roughly 300 attendees of Helgoland 2025. After cleaning, the survey retained 1,101 usable responses (about a 7% response rate).

This was not a referendum of all physicists and not an experiment capable of deciding an interpretation. It was a voluntary survey of a deliberately quantum-adjacent research sample: 68% identified as theorists, 17% as experimentalists and 13% as both; 19% selected quantum foundations or philosophy among their fields. People who care about the question are likely over-represented. The results describe this respondent pool, not "what physics has proved" or a universal disciplinary consensus.

## The Useful Numbers

Among the 1,101 respondents who selected an overall interpretation:

- 36.1% selected the survey's broad "Copenhagen interpretation" label.
- 16.6% selected epistemic/information-based approaches.
- 15.0% selected the combined Everett/many-worlds/consistent-histories option.
- 6.6% selected Bohm-de Broglie pilot-wave theory; 3.7% spontaneous collapse; and 3.5% relational quantum mechanics.
- 42.9% said they were **not confident** that their selected interpretation was correct; 32.9% were fairly confident and 24.3% confident.

The last figure is the important corrective to "Copenhagen won": it was the largest single answer, not a settled account of what exists.

The survey's grouping also needs its own health warning. *Nature* corrected the feature on 12 August 2025 because it had grouped many-worlds and consistent histories together as though both involved branching worlds; consistent histories does not. The questionnaire itself describes "Copenhagen" as a popular umbrella rather than a self-consistent interpretation. These labels are practical survey buckets, not precision-engineered philosophical taxonomy.

## The Wavefunction Split

Of the 1,014 respondents who answered the wavefunction question:

- 46.8% called it a mathematical tool for predicting outcomes and probabilities, "and nothing else".
- 18.7% called it a partial representation of physical reality.
- 17.1% called it a complete representation of physical reality.
- 7.7% called it a subjective description of beliefs about outcomes; 7.8% chose another account.

So yes: the working physicists in this sample do disagree about whether the wavefunction is ontology, partial ontology, belief/information, or calculation machinery. What they do not disagree about is the predictive success of quantum mechanics. The disagreement is about how, or whether, the formalism maps onto an underlying reality.

## Why It Belongs Here

This survey gives the existing [Interpretations](../../themes/interpretations.md) and [Rovelli, Relational Quantum Mechanics, and Reality](../../themes/rovelli-relational-quantum-mechanics-and-reality.md) package a useful empirical-social fact: the interpretive plurality in those pages is not merely a tidy textbook menu. Even a large, deliberately relevant research sample had no dominant confidence-backed answer.

It also supports the framing in [Quantum Interpretation Completion Schemes](../concepts/quantum-interpretation-completion-schemes.md): several programmes can accommodate much of the same successful operational formalism while making very different commitments about states, facts, collapse, hidden variables, branches, observers, and relations. The survey does not show that all interpretations are equally good, or that evidence can never distinguish them. It shows that no consensus has yet made the choice boring.

## Do Not Overclaim

- Do not say the survey proves reality is subjective, observer-created, or unknowable.
- Do not say Copenhagen is "right" because it received the most responses.
- Do not treat the wavefunction question as a simple real-versus-fake switch: views differ over what sort of thing it could be, what it represents, and which physical description is fundamental.
- Do not use the poll as evidence for manifestation, consciousness-causes-collapse, many-worlds, or relational quantum mechanics. A survey records views; it is not a Bell test wearing a lanyard.
