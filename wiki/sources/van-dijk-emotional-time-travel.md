# Van Dijk et al. - Emotional Time Travel

## Why This Matters

Van Dijk, van Dillen, Seip, and Rotteveel's magnificently titled 2012 paper adds a regulation mechanism to affective forecasting. People may overestimate future anger and sadness not only because they imagine the upsetting event too vividly, but because they under-imagine what their future mind will do after it happens.

The useful sequence is:

**specified negative event -> imagined emotion without enough future regulation -> overestimated emotional impact**

This is adjacent to [Morewedge, Gilbert, and Wilson's memory-sampling account](morewedge-least-likely-of-times.md), but it is not the same explanation. Morewedge asks which past episode becomes the forecast's input. Van Dijk asks whether the forecast includes the later reappraisal processes that will change the emotional response.

## The 2012 Study

The study compared people forecasting their reactions to social exclusion with people actually experiencing exclusion. Forecasters predicted more intense anger and sadness than experiencers reported. The difference appeared both in overall emotion ratings and in associated response-tendency measures.

Forecasters also expected less reappraisal than experiencers actually used. The publisher abstract identifies **relativisation**--placing the exclusion in a broader or less absolute frame--as the clearest specific result: among experiencers, more relativisation was associated with less anger. Predicted relativisation was not related to forecasters' predicted anger.

That gives the impact bias a more concrete mechanism than "people are bad at feelings." Future distress is simulated as though the emotional appraisal will remain fixed. Actual people continue processing the event.

## The 2017 Guilt And Shame Follow-Up

The same authors later tested a related mechanism with 52 female undergraduates. Participants either imagined or experienced a contrived task failure in which their poor score also cost another participant a bonus. Forecasters predicted more guilt and shame than experiencers reported.

They also predicted less acceptance and more rumination than experiencers actually used. In the authors' mediation analyses, those acceptance and rumination differences statistically accounted for the forecast-versus-experience differences in guilt and shame; suppression did not. The reappraisal scale had poor internal consistency and was excluded from the main analyses.

This is useful supporting evidence because it extends the mechanism across different negative emotions and names both a down-regulating process (acceptance) and an up-regulating one (rumination). It is a related conceptual follow-up, not a large preregistered replication of the 2012 exclusion study.

## What It Adds To The Wiki

Together with Morewedge, this produces a two-error model of emotional prediction:

1. **Input error:** an extreme or otherwise unrepresentative memory is selected as evidence.
2. **Process error:** the future emotional system is imagined without enough of the regulation and reappraisal it will actually perform.

The practical question is therefore not merely "How bad will this feel?" It is:

- Which past case am I using as my template?
- Am I treating that case as representative?
- What will future-me do with the event after the first emotional hit?
- Am I forecasting an emotion, or forecasting an emotion frozen in amber?

This does not mean future anger or sadness is imaginary. It means initial emotional force and later regulated experience are different targets.

## Later Context

Rizeq's 2024 scoping review places affective forecasting inside a heterogeneous and still methodologically unsettled literature. Forecast intensity is associated with several forms of psychopathology, but studies define and measure forecasting in different ways, and normative levels are not established. That supports keeping the mechanism as a useful research tile rather than turning it into a universal law about resilience.

## Limits

- The 2012 article is closed access. Wiley and the University of Amsterdam expose metadata and the abstract, but no openly licensed full text was identified in this research pass. This note therefore does not invent unexposed sample or procedural details.
- The 2012 result came from one social-exclusion paradigm and self-report measures.
- Associations between reappraisal and emotional intensity do not by themselves prove that reappraisal caused the whole forecasting gap.
- The 2017 follow-up had 52 female undergraduates, used deception and a contrived laboratory event, and was not preregistered.
- The 2017 mediation is statistical evidence consistent with the mechanism, not proof of a complete causal chain.
- Emotion regulation is not always beneficial or accurate. Reappraisal can clarify an event, minimise it, excuse it, or become avoidance depending on context.
