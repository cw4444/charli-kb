# Oxford Tutorial System

## Summary

Oxford describes its undergraduate tutorial as a small-group meeting—normally one to three students with a tutor—built around prepared work on a particular topic. A tutor sets the next task; the student spends substantial time working independently; then the work is discussed, challenged, and fed back on. In humanities this often means an essay, while sciences commonly use problem sheets.

The important mechanism is not simply "small class size." A bounded question gives the independent work a target, and the tutorial makes the student state, substantiate, revise, and communicate a view in front of someone able to notice where the argument is woolly.

## What Oxford Explicitly Says

- Tutorials normally run for about an hour, often once or twice weekly, with one to three students and a tutor.
- Students prepare independently from a reading list or other guidance, then receive feedback on the resulting essay, problem sheet, or other work.
- Students are expected to present and substantiate opinions, accept constructive criticism, listen, and assess thinking for clarity, precision, depth, breadth, and relevance.
- Oxford's public tutorial-preparation guide gives a particularly useful first move for essay work: deconstruct the title by asking what questions it contains and what its terms mean.

## Portable Lesson, Not Oxford Costume

The [Question-Led Inquiry](../concepts/question-led-inquiry.md) method takes the durable part outside a university: let a real question constrain the search; make a provisional answer; deliberately seek the strongest contrary case; then expose the argument to criticism. This is an adaptation, not a claim that every Oxford tutor requires a fixed "research the opposite" ritual.

Nor is this an excuse to cosplay a degree in a shed. The full Oxford system also depends on subject expertise, a curriculum, libraries, peers, and sustained tutor feedback. A self-directed version needs an honest substitute for the last part: a knowledgeable person, a well-chosen critical source, or a written audit that can actually prove the answer wrong.
