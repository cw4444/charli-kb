# Pagani et al. - Autism Subtypes And Cross-Species Connectivity

Marco Pagani, Valerio Zerbi, Silvia Gini, Adriana Di Martino, Alessandro Gozzi, and colleagues published "Autism subtypes identified using cross-species functional connectivity analyses" in *Nature Neuroscience* on 2026-05-15. Nature marks the article open access under CC BY 4.0.

The paper asks whether heterogeneity in autism can be connected to different underlying biology rather than treated as one averaged brain pattern. The authors use cross-species resting-state fMRI: first clustering dysconnectivity in mouse models carrying autism-relevant genetic or immune perturbations, then using those mouse-derived patterns to identify analogous human fMRI subtypes.

## What They Did

The rodent side used resting-state fMRI across 20 mouse models relevant to autism biology. These were not "autistic mice" in the human diagnostic sense. They were mice with specific mutations or immune-related manipulations linked to autism-relevant pathways, each compared with wild-type littermate controls.

The human side used low-motion resting-state fMRI from 940 autistic people aged 5-30 and 1,036 neurotypical controls across 38 data collections, mostly from ABIDE plus an additional Child Mind Institute sample. The authors split the data into discovery and replication sets before testing subtype reproducibility.

## What They Found

In mice, functional-connectivity alterations clustered into two dominant patterns:

- a hypoconnectivity-dominant subtype, associated mainly with synaptic dysfunction;
- a hyperconnectivity-dominant subtype, associated mainly with transcriptional and immune-related alterations.

Using cross-species regions with plausible anatomical and functional correspondence, the authors then identified analogous human subtypes. In the aggregated human autism dataset, the two subtypes accounted for about one-quarter of scans: 74 people in the hypoconnectivity subtype and 162 in the hyperconnectivity subtype. Most autistic participants in the dataset were not assigned to either subtype.

The two human subtypes had different functional network architectures. The hyperconnectivity subtype showed increased subcortical-cortical connectivity and reduced cortico-cortical connectivity among temporoparietal, visual and somatomotor networks. The hypoconnectivity subtype showed reduced connectivity involving somatomotor and temporoparietal networks. The hyperconnectivity subtype also had moderately higher ADOS calibrated severity scores and higher social-affect scores, but not significantly different restricted/repetitive-behaviour scores.

Gene-decoding analyses suggested that the human hypoconnectivity subtype recapitulated synaptic pathway enrichment, while the hyperconnectivity subtype recapitulated immune-related pathway enrichment. The authors frame this as evidence that some autism-related fMRI heterogeneity may map onto dissociable biological pathways.

## Why It Matters

The paper is useful because it refuses the usual flattening move. Autism is not treated as one average deviation from neurotypical controls. Instead, the authors try to connect different connectivity patterns to different biological pathways.

For the wiki, the important bridge is heterogeneity. A spectrum is not just "more or less autism." It may contain different routes into superficially overlapping diagnostic territory. That matters for research design, intervention studies, and avoiding one-size-fits-all stories.

The cross-species piece is also interesting for mechanism. Mouse models cannot contain human autistic experience, identity, masking, language history, social trauma, or culture. But they can isolate specific genetic or immune mechanisms and ask whether those mechanisms produce conserved network-level signatures.

## Caveats

- The authors explicitly say the subtypes are observational and not ready for clinical use.
- The two identified human subtypes covered only about one-quarter of the autistic scans; most participants were not assigned to either.
- Resting-state fMRI connectivity is not a diagnostic test and should not be treated as a personal identity readout.
- Mouse models are mechanism models, not miniature autistic people.
- Rodents cannot model common polygenic autism in a clean way, and they cannot model human language, masking, social context, or lived experience.
- The hyperconnectivity/hypoconnectivity split is coarse; additional subtypes may exist.
- The sample was limited to ages 5-30 and low-motion scans, which can bias who is represented.
- Sex-balanced cohorts and further validation are still needed.
