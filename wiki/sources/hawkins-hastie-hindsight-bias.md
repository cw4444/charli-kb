# Hawkins And Hastie - Hindsight Bias

## Source

Scott A. Hawkins and Reid Hastie, [Hindsight: Biased Judgments of Past Events After the Outcomes Are Known](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.107.3.311), *Psychological Bulletin*, 107(3), 311-327, 1990.

Access note: APA journal article; this note uses the public abstract and bibliographic record rather than reproducing the copyrighted review.

## Why It Matters

Hindsight bias is the tendency for people who know an outcome to overestimate how well they would have predicted it beforehand. The past becomes easier to explain after the uncertainty has been removed, and that ease can be misremembered as prior foresight.

Hawkins and Hastie's review matters because it does not treat the effect as one simple memory failure. It organizes empirical explanations around several points at which outcome knowledge can alter judgment.

## The Mechanism Stack

The review describes at least four broad routes:

1. **Evidence selection:** knowing the outcome changes which antecedent facts are retrieved or treated as relevant.
2. **Evidence evaluation:** outcome-consistent facts appear more diagnostic or important than they did beforehand.
3. **Evidence integration:** selected facts are assembled into a more coherent causal account of the known result.
4. **Response generation:** the outcome can influence how a person translates their reconstructed understanding into a probability or verbal answer.

Earlier accounts called the broad effect **creeping determinism**: the outcome becomes integrated into the person's representation of the event, making what happened feel more inevitable. Hawkins and Hastie's important correction is that no single route has to explain every case. Several mechanisms may combine, and different tasks can produce the same surface bias by different routes.

## Why This Damages Learning

Outcome feedback should help people improve their models. Hindsight bias can interfere by rewriting the baseline:

- warning signs that fit the outcome now look obvious;
- alternatives that were plausible beforehand lose visibility;
- surprise is converted into apparent inevitability;
- people may blame decision-makers for failing to foresee what only became clear later;
- a lucky decision can receive more credit than its original reasoning deserved.

The practical problem is not merely inaccurate memory. If the mind cannot recover what uncertainty actually looked like, it cannot cleanly distinguish a good process with a bad outcome from a bad process that got lucky.

## Important Distinction

Hindsight bias and outcome bias are related but not identical.

- **Hindsight bias:** "I would have predicted that outcome; it was foreseeable."
- **Outcome bias:** "Because the result was bad, the decision itself must have been bad."

The first distorts remembered predictability. The second judges decision quality through the result. Both can corrupt retrospective review, but they are not interchangeable labels.

## Evidence Boundary

- This is a 1990 narrative review of the evidence available at the time, not a modern preregistered meta-analysis.
- The review's four routes are an organizing framework, not proof that every hindsight judgment uses all four.
- Outcome knowledge can legitimately improve understanding. Bias enters when present knowledge is projected backward into what could reasonably have been known then.
- Hindsight bias need not be deliberate self-aggrandizement or dishonesty. Reconstructive memory and causal sense-making can produce it without conscious intent.
