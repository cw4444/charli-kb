# Moretto et al. - Tau, Axonal Transport, And p38α

This source note covers Edoardo Moretto and colleagues' 2026 *Nature Neuroscience* paper, "Aberrant tau accumulation caused by MAPT mutations induces early pathological changes in axonal transport that are rescued by p38α inhibition."

## Metadata

- Journal: *Nature Neuroscience*
- Published: 2026-06-04
- DOI: <https://doi.org/10.1038/s41593-026-02266-4>
- Access: open access, CC BY 4.0
- Subjects: cellular neuroscience, dementia, membrane trafficking, molecular neuroscience

## Core Finding

The paper studies axonal transport in mouse cortex using two-photon imaging of brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) granules.

The authors report that:

- axonal transport deficits appear early in tau pathology;
- these deficits occur before neurofibrillary tangle formation and neuronal death;
- tau accumulation enlarges tau envelopes on microtubules;
- the enlarged tau envelopes act as functional barriers to cargo transport;
- inhibiting MAPK p38α reverses the transport deficits in the studied models.

The useful mechanism is not simply "tau is bad." Tau pathology appears able to disrupt the physical logistics system of the axon before the later pathological landmarks people usually notice.

## Why Axonal Transport Matters

Neurons have an awkward engineering problem. They can be extremely long, but the cell body still has to support distant axons and synapses with proteins, organelles, signaling molecules, and other cargo.

Microtubules act as intracellular tracks. Motor proteins and cargoes move along them. If pathological tau forms barriers on those tracks, the neuron can become functionally impaired before it dies.

That makes axonal transport a useful bridge between molecular pathology and circuit failure: a neuron can still be present while its internal supply lines are already failing.

## Why p38α Matters

p38α is a mitogen-activated protein kinase involved in stress and inflammatory signaling. The paper reports that p38α inhibition restored axonal transport in the studied tauopathy models.

This is interesting because it suggests the early transport deficit is not necessarily a one-way consequence of irreversible structural destruction.

It is not evidence that p38α inhibition cures Alzheimer's disease, frontotemporal dementia, or tauopathies generally. The paper establishes a mechanistic and preclinical intervention signal in mouse models.

## Why It Matters Here

This paper gives the new [Neuroscience](../../themes/neuroscience/overview.md) lane a lower-level cellular anchor.

The wiki already tracks how brains classify experience, imagine futures, update beliefs, and support agency. This source is a reminder that all of that depends on physical neurons maintaining transport, structure, signaling, and metabolic life.

The broader bridge is useful:

- cognition is not separate from cellular logistics;
- early dysfunction can precede obvious cell death;
- mechanism matters because different failure stages may have different reversibility;
- "the brain" is not one thing but a stack from molecules and transport to circuits, experience, belief, and action.

## Do Not Overclaim

- This is a mouse-model study, not a human clinical trial.
- Reversal of axonal transport deficits is not reversal of dementia.
- p38α is involved in many biological processes; inhibiting it is not automatically safe or specific enough for treatment.
- Tauopathies are heterogeneous, and a mechanism demonstrated in the studied models may not explain every disease stage or patient.
- Early transport dysfunction preceding tangles and neuronal death does not mean all later pathology is avoidable.
