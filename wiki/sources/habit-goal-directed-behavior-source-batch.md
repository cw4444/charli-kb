# Habit, Goal-Directed Action, And Behavioural Control Source Batch

## The Useful Distinction

This research tradition separates two ways an action can be controlled:

- **Habitual control:** a learned cue-response pattern that can run efficiently and automatically, but may persist after the outcome has lost value or the situation has changed.
- **Goal-directed control:** action guided by the current expected value of its outcome, allowing a person or animal to alter course when the outcome, contingency, or context changes.

The distinction is not a morality play. Habits reduce decision burden and make ordinary life possible; goal-directed control is costly and does not need to manually supervise every tooth-brushing operation. The real problem is switching: can current goals regain control when a cached response has become wrong for the present situation?

## Historical Anchor: Dickinson's Behavioural Autonomy

Anthony Dickinson's 1985 paper, *Actions and habits: The development of behavioural autonomy*, is the early conceptual anchor for this lane. It described how behaviour can become autonomous from the outcome that originally trained it: an action may continue because the cue-response relation has acquired control, rather than because the actor is currently pursuing the outcome.

The paper's charmingly compact affiliation is the University of Cambridge, CB2 2EB. Academia was apparently still allowed to be a postcard then.

The later literature operationalizes this idea with **outcome devaluation** and **contingency degradation**:

- If an action falls after its reward is made undesirable, it remains sensitive to the current outcome and counts as goal-directed in that task.
- If it continues unchanged after devaluation, or after the action no longer reliably produces the reward, it is treated as more habitual.

The original 1985 article is cited in the open Mendelsohn commentary below. This batch does not claim a public full text for it.

## Circuit Evidence: Gremel And Costa 2013

Gremel and Costa used an outcome-revaluation lever-press task in mice where the same animal could use a goal-directed or habitual strategy in different reinforcement contexts. Their findings support a dynamic corticostriatal account:

- dorsomedial striatum (DMS) and orbitofrontal cortex (OFC) were more engaged when pressing was sensitive to changed outcome value;
- dorsolateral striatum (DLS) was relatively more engaged under the habitual strategy;
- inhibiting OFC disrupted goal-directed responding after revaluation, while OFC stimulation altered goal-directed pressing.

This is useful mechanism evidence, not a map of human willpower. It says that outcome-sensitive action depends on particular mouse circuits in a particular task; it does not say people can zap their OFC into becoming organised.

## Human Measurement Brake: de Wit et al. 2018

de Wit and colleagues reported five failed attempts to produce stronger habitual responding through extensive instrumental training in healthy humans. Their result matters because it complicates the easy story that repetition simply converts any behaviour into a habit on schedule.

The authors' interpretation is especially useful for the clinical literature: poor adjustment after outcome devaluation may reflect weak goal-directed control rather than a supercharged habit system. In plain English, an apparently stubborn action does not tell you by itself whether the habit engine is too strong, the goal-updating system is underpowered, or the task failed to measure the distinction cleanly.

## Molecular Substrate: Lovinger 2010

David Lovinger's 2010 review supplies the lower-level machinery beneath the DMS/DLS and OFC control story. The dorsal striatum is not a single habit button; it is a dense learning-and-action system in which medium spiny neurons integrate cortical and thalamic glutamate input with dopamine, acetylcholine, GABA, adenosine, and endocannabinoid modulation.

The review's useful broad map is:

- glutamatergic LTP in striatal projection neurons involves NMDA receptors plus D1 dopamine or A2A adenosine signalling;
- a major form of glutamatergic LTD uses metabotropic glutamate receptors, D2 dopamine signalling, and retrograde endocannabinoid/CB1 mechanisms;
- DMS and DLS differ in their learning roles, while direct and indirect pathway neurons have partly distinct plasticity conditions;
- receptor manipulations and lesions in animal tasks point to separable molecular contributions to action-outcome learning, habit learning, extinction, and skilled action sequences.

This is a review, mostly synthesizing animal, slice, lesion, and pharmacological work available by 2010. It gives the habit lane a mechanism floor rather than a fresh room: no conclusion here licenses a human dopamine hack, a cannabinoid protocol, or a diagnosis of why somebody keeps opening the fridge.

## Prospective Planning: Schmidt, Wikenheiser, And Redish 2018

This is not the whole edited volume. It is the 32-page PDF of Chapter 6, *Goal-Directed Sequences in the Hippocampus*, from the larger 2018 book *Goal-Directed Decision Making: Computations and Neural Circuits*.

Its useful contribution is the hippocampal complement to the striatal story. A goal-directed decision may require more than updating the value of a learned action: it can involve constructing possible future paths through a cognitive map, comparing their likely consequences, and choosing under uncertainty.

The chapter gathers several evidence lines:

- **Vicarious trial and error (VTE):** pauses and back-and-forth looking at choice points can mark deliberation, especially in novel or difficult decisions; they are not a direct thought readout.
- **Hippocampal theta sequences:** within a theta cycle, place-cell activity can represent locations ahead of the animal, including alternate routes at decision points. This is consistent with prospective simulation, although the exact causal role remains unresolved.
- **Sharp-wave ripples (SWRs):** replay-like hippocampal events may support memory retrieval and planning; disrupting waking SWRs can impair working-memory performance in animal work, but does not prove that every ripple is a conscious plan.
- **Interaction with prefrontal cortex:** deliberative behaviour is treated as a network process involving hippocampal map-like representations and prefrontal evaluation/control, rather than a hippocampus-only imagination organ.

For this wiki, the clean bridge is: goal-directed action can mean using a learned model to simulate routes and outcomes before acting. That belongs beside, not above, the striatal cue-response and plasticity material. Planning, cached skills, context, action value, and bodily control are different jobs in the same extremely unhelpful committee.

## Why It Matters Here

This is the neuroscience underneath several existing wiki interests:

- [Optimism](../concepts/optimism.md): a hopeful future expectation only changes life through attention, choices, persistence, and updating; it does not get executive status by vibes alone.
- [Manifestation As Behavior Modification](../concepts/manifestation-as-behavior-modification.md): useful future rehearsal must meet cues, routines, and corrective feedback in the actual environment.
- [Fictional Finalism](../concepts/fictional-finalism.md): imagined future goals can organise present action, but they must repeatedly regain control from context-cued defaults.
- [Practical Agency Inside Constraint](../concepts/practical-agency-inside-constraint.md): agency includes noticing when a response has become cached, checking whether the outcome still matters, and redesigning the cue-action arrangement.

## Do Not Overclaim

- Do not call every repeated behaviour a laboratory-defined habit.
- Do not turn DMS, DLS, or OFC into cartoon "purpose," "habit," or "willpower" buttons.
- Do not infer human diagnosis, treatment, ADHD, OCD, addiction, or personal moral failure from a cue-response framework.
- Do not confuse a goal with a method: a desired future can be real while the routine supposedly serving it is obsolete.
- Do not claim that stress makes people irrational. It can shift control toward lower-cost, practiced strategies; that is sometimes adaptive and sometimes disastrously context-blind.
