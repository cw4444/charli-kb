# Wigner's Friend

Wigner's friend is a thought experiment about applying quantum mechanics to an observer.

A friend inside a sealed lab measures a quantum system and records a definite outcome. Wigner, outside the sealed lab, can in principle treat the whole lab, including the friend and record, as a quantum system evolving unitarily.

The tension is simple:

- For the friend, an outcome happened.
- For Wigner, before opening or measuring the lab, the lab may be described as a superposition.

## Why This Is Hard

Ordinary quantum mechanics uses two kinds of update:

- Unitary evolution: smooth, reversible evolution of the quantum state.
- Collapse/update: an outcome is registered and the state is updated.

Wigner's friend asks whether collapse is objective, observer-relative, merely informational, or not fundamental at all.

Many-worlds is one of the cleanest responses because it keeps universal unitary evolution and denies that there must be one unique global outcome. See [Many-Worlds, Wheeler, and Observer-Dependent Reality](many-worlds-and-observer-dependent-reality.md).

## Extended Wigner's Friend

Extended Wigner's-friend scenarios put multiple friends and superobservers into a larger protocol. This lets theorists import Bell-style reasoning:

- two separated labs,
- choices between measuring a friend's record or measuring the whole lab coherently,
- comparison of correlations,
- no-go conclusions if certain assumptions are imposed.

Frauchiger-Renner and Brukner are different but related versions of this move.

## Friend Does Not Need To Be Conscious

Modern versions usually do not require consciousness. A "friend" can be an information-recording system, a detector, a memory, or another quantum system that stores a result. The philosophical stakes are larger if the friend is a human, but the formal argument is operational.

## Why It Matters

The Wigner's-friend setup creates pressure on the idea that there is always one public fact of the matter about every measurement outcome. It does not automatically show that everyday macroscopic facts are unstable. It shows that universal quantum mechanics plus ordinary fact-talk can clash in carefully designed scenarios.
